Router-caused failures: the 2026-11-20 fallback audit

Finding: the fallback chain was causing much of what it then routed around.

Measured over 353 live trajectory records (2384 stages, 2026-11-12 to 2026-11-20), at least 63 of 573
failed stages were the router corrupting a valid client request. Each was booked against the provider and
quarantined, and `key_rejected` costs a single strike — so healthy providers were evicted from the chain
on the strength of the router's own bug.

The six classes and their live counts:
  1. AUTH_RE's bare `auth` alternative matched completion prose.       45 stages (200 OK -> "auth")
  2. buildFailoverPreservedBody appended a trailing system message.    21 failures (Z.ai code 1214)
  3. max_tokens never clamped to the target's outputTokens.            30 failures (943718 > 131072)
  4. prompt_cache_key / store reached Google's strict schema.         ~40 failures (Unknown name)
  5. estimateRequestContext folded max_tokens into context need.       30 records (req 945635)
  6. No-dial models hard-excluded when a level was requested.         167 of 200 requests

All six are fixed and the fixes are verified — see
`.agents/plans/2026-11-20-16-fallback-tooling-audit.plan.md`.

Why it matters beyond these six: class 1 in particular is the shape of failure that makes every future
audit harder. A misclassification does not just route one request wrong; it poisons the health registry
and it makes the trajectory log — the artifact an operator or agent reads to decide what is broken —
report the opposite of the truth. Any classifier change should be replayed against the live log before it
is trusted, which is now cheap: the outbound body for every stage is recorded, so a misclassification can
be reproduced from the record alone.

Standing lesson: when a failure is classified against a provider, the record must contain what was SENT.
Without the outbound body there is no way to tell a provider fault from a router fault after the fact, and
the natural assumption — the provider failed — is the wrong one often enough to matter (11% of failures
here).
